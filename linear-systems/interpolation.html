Why linear systems? Fitting a curve through data

Ask for the polynomial that passes exactly through your data points and you get one linear equation per point. The unknowns are the polynomial’s coefficients. Drag the points and watch the system change.

What to notice

  1. A depends only on where you measure; b is the data. Move a point straight up or down and only b changes. Press “New data, same x’s” a few times: same sensor locations, new readings, a new system with the identical matrix. Solving from scratch every time throws away work. Factoring A once is the idea behind LU.
  2. The number of points sets the size of the system. With n points you solve an n×n system. Real problems (a simulation grid, an image, a network) have n in the thousands or millions, which is why cost matters.
  3. Some choices of x make a bad matrix. Press “Crowd the x’s together” and watch cond(A) jump: the rows become nearly identical, and a tiny nudge of the data swings the curve wildly. That is conditioning, and you can see it before you measure anything.
Where else does Ax = b show up?
  • Least squares (more data than unknowns) reduces to a square system; see the least-squares explorer.
  • Heat, stress, flow: discretize a differential equation on a grid and every grid point contributes one equation. The heated rod is the smallest example.
  • Newton’s method for systems and implicit ODE solvers solve a linear system at every single step.
