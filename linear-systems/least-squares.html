Least squares, the normal equations, and QR

With more data points than unknowns, Ax = b usually has no solution. Least squares picks the x that makes the residual r = b − Ax as short as possible. This page shows what that means in a picture, why the answer is unique, and why the way you compute it matters.

The “least squares”, drawn as actual squares

Each residual ri is the vertical gap between a data point and the line. Least squares minimizes ∑ ri², the total area of the squares drawn on those gaps. Try to beat it by hand.

The geometry: drop a perpendicular

As x varies, Ax sweeps out the column space C(A): every combination of A’s columns. With two columns it is a plane through the origin. b sits off that plane, and least squares asks which point of the plane is closest to b. The answer is the foot of the perpendicular, b̂ = Ax*.

Move the other point y = Ac around the plane: it is always farther from b, by exactly the Pythagorean theorem.

bb̂ and the residualanother point y

“The residual is perpendicular to the plane” means it is perpendicular to every column of A: AT(b − Ax*) = 0. Rearranged, that is the normal equations ATAx* = ATb: the same equations calculus gives when you set the gradient of ‖Ax − b‖² to zero.

The theorem: exactly one best fit

Theorem. If A (m×n, m ≥ n) has full column rank, then min ‖Ax − b‖² has exactly one minimizer, x* = (ATA)−1ATb.

    Normal equations vs. QR

    In exact arithmetic the normal equations and QR give the same answer. In floating point, forming ATA squares the condition number: cond(ATA) = cond(A)². Here a polynomial is fit through 14 equally spaced points on [0, 1]. The data are generated from known coefficients with no noise, so any error in the recovered coefficients is pure rounding.

    normal equationsQR

    QR loses digits in proportion to cond(A). The normal equations lose them in proportion to cond(A)², twice as many digits. Past degree 10, cond(ATA) passes 1016 and the normal equations lose every digit, even though A itself is nowhere near singular.

    Building Q and R: Gram–Schmidt

    QR replaces the columns of A with an orthonormal set q1, q2, … spanning the same space, and records the bookkeeping in an upper-triangular R. Projecting onto orthonormal vectors needs only dot products, which is why the least-squares answer then drops out of Rx = QTb with no ATA in sight.
