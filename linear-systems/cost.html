What LU costs, and why not the inverse

Factoring an n×n matrix costs about ⅔n³ flops; each triangular solve after that costs about n². This page shows where those numbers come from, what they mean in seconds, and why computing A−1 loses on cost, accuracy and memory.

Where ⅔n³ comes from

At step k, elimination updates every entry of the block below and to the right of the pivot: an (n−k)×(n−k) block, with one multiply and one subtract per entry. The block shrinks by one row and one column each step.

Stack the updated blocks on top of each other and they form a stepped pyramid with an n×n base. A pyramid holds ⅓ of its bounding box, so the blocks contain about n³/3 entries, and at 2 flops per entry that is ⅔n³. The same idea gives the triangular solves: row i of forward substitution touches i entries, a triangle of about n²/2 entries, so about n² flops per solve.

Why not compute A−1?

“Compute A−1 once, then every new b is just a multiply” sounds efficient. LU already gives you that reuse, and the inverse loses in three separate ways.

1. Cost

Computing A−1 costs about 2n³ flops, three times the factorization, and multiplying A−1b costs 2n², the same as a forward-plus-back solve. So you pay triple up front and save nothing per right-hand side (see the table above).

2. A one-number version

Solve 49x = 49 two ways in floating point: divide directly, or form the “inverse” 1/49 and multiply.

x = b / a
x = (1/a) × b

Division rounds once. The inverse route rounds twice: once when storing 1/a, again after multiplying. A matrix inverse repeats that extra rounding in every one of its n² entries.

3. Accuracy

Take the Hilbert matrix Hij = 1/(i+j−1), a classic ill-conditioned test case, and b = H·(1, …, 1). Solve with pivoted LU, and again with inv(H) @ b.

Both answers sit about equally far from the true x: that error is set by cond(H), a property of the problem, which neither method can beat. The difference is the residual ‖b − Hx‖/‖b‖. The LU answer satisfies the equations to machine precision: it is the exact solution of a problem within rounding of yours. The inverse’s answer does not, and the gap grows with cond(H).

4. Memory: the heated rod

The steady temperature along a rod, computed at n grid points, satisfies −Ti−1 + 2Ti − Ti+1 = (heat source at point i). Each point talks only to its two neighbours, so A is tridiagonal: almost every entry is zero. Compare what LU and the inverse do with those zeros.

L and U keep the band: storing and solving with them is proportional to n, not n² or n³. The inverse has no zeros at all, because heat at any point affects the temperature everywhere. At n = 10⁶ grid points (modest for 2D or 3D problems), the tridiagonal A needs about 24 MB, while A−1 would need 8 TB.
