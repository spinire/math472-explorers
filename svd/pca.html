The best line through a cloud

Least squares minimizes vertical distances, which treats x as exact. When both coordinates are noisy, the fairer choice is the perpendicular distance. After centering the data, the SVD’s first direction v1 gives the line with the smallest total squared perpendicular distance. That is Eckart–Young, and it is the first step of principal component analysis.

Try to beat the v1 line

Every line through the mean gives a rank-one approximation of the centered data: replace each point by its projection onto the line. The error of that approximation is the line’s total squared perpendicular distance. Turn your line and watch that total. Eckart–Young says it can never drop below σ22.

Total squared perpendicular distance to a line through the mean, against the line’s angle. The lowest point is the v1 direction, where the total is σ22; the highest is the v2 direction, where it is σ12.

Things to try

  • Both readings noisy. The two least-squares lines fan out on either side of the v1 line. Which one would you use to convert one thermometer’s reading into the other’s?
  • Only y noisy. Now x really is exact. Which line should you trust here, and why does the v1 line come out too steep?
  • No clear direction. σ1 and σ2 are close, the curve above is nearly flat, and v1 swings wildly when you nudge one point. When is the first principal direction worth reporting?
  • Drag one point far away from the others. Which line moves most?
