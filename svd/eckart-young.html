Eckart–Young in the 2-norm

Cutting the SVD after k layers gives the best possible rank-k approximation, and its error in the 2-norm is exactly σk+1. The short proof comes first. After it, each piece of the proof gets an example you can drag and test.

The theorem

Take A to be n × n for simplicity (the same argument works for any shape), and write its SVD as a sum of rank-one layers, with the singular values in decreasing order. Let \(A_k\) keep the first k of them:

\[ A=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_i u_i v_i^T,\qquad \sigma_1\ge\cdots\ge\sigma_n\ge 0,\qquad A_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sigma_i u_i v_i^T . \]

The error \(A-A_k\) is the sum of the discarded layers, so its largest stretch is \(\sigma_{k+1}\). The theorem says no other rank-k matrix does better.

Theorem (Eckart–Young, 2-norm). For each \(k<n\) and every matrix B of rank at most k,

\[ \|A-B\|_2\;\ge\;\sigma_{k+1}=\|A-A_k\|_2 . \]

Here \(\|M\|_2\) is the largest stretch M gives to a unit vector: the largest value of \(\|Mx\|\) over all x with \(\|x\|=1\).

The proof

The plan is to find one unit vector w that B sends to zero and A stretches by at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\). That is enough: on such a w the error \(A-B\) acts exactly like A, and the largest stretch of \(A-B\) is at least the stretch of w. Let B be any matrix of rank at most k, and let S be the span of \(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\).

Step 1 (A is strong on S). A unit vector in S is \(w=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_{k+1}v_{k+1}\) with \(c_1^2+\cdots+c_{k+1}^2=1\), because the v’s are orthonormal. Since \(Av_i=\sigma_iu_i\), the \(u_i\) are orthonormal, and \(\sigma_i\ge\sigma_{k+1}\) for every \(i\le k+1\),

\[ \|Aw\|^2=c_1^2\sigma_1^2+\cdots+c_{k+1}^2\sigma_{k+1}^2\;\ge\;\sigma_{k+1}^2\,(c_1^2+\cdots+c_{k+1}^2)=\sigma_{k+1}^2 . \]

Step 2 (B sends some w in S to zero). Every output of B lies in its column space, which has dimension at most k. The \(k+1\) vectors \(Bv_1,\dots,Bv_{k+1}\) therefore cannot be independent: there are numbers \(c_1,\dots,c_{k+1}\), not all zero, with

\[ c_1Bv_1+\cdots+c_{k+1}Bv_{k+1}=0 . \]

The left side is \(B(c_1v_1+\cdots+c_{k+1}v_{k+1})\). So \(w=c_1v_1+\cdots+c_{k+1}v_{k+1}\) is a vector in S with \(Bw=0\). It is not zero, because the v’s are independent, so we can rescale it to length 1.

Conclusion. For this w, \((A-B)w=Aw\), so by Step 1

\[ \|A-B\|_2\;\ge\;\|(A-B)w\|=\|Aw\|\;\ge\;\sigma_{k+1}. \qquad\blacksquare \]

The first inequality holds because \(\|A-B\|_2\) is the largest stretch over all unit vectors, so it is at least the stretch of this one.

The three ingredients, one example each

The proof rests on three facts. Each is small, and each has a picture.

One unit vector is enough to bound a largest stretch (the conclusion)

The conclusion bounds \(\|A-B\|_2\) below by the stretch of a single vector. That works because \(\|A-B\|_2\) is a largest stretch: one input stretched by \(\sigma_{k+1}\) shows the largest is at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\), and inputs with smaller stretch cannot lower it.

Example. \(M=\operatorname{diag}(0,2)\) sends (1, 0) to zero, a stretch of 0. Its 2-norm is still 2, because (0, 1) is stretched by 2:

\[ M\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad M\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|M\|_2=2 . \]

The order matters too. B is chosen first, by an opponent who may make it as clever as they like; all we know about it is that its rank is at most k. Then we must find w for that B. We never get to inspect B and pick a convenient vector, so the argument must work for every B.

A stretches every vector in S by at least σk+1 (Step 1)

Step 1 is split, stretch, add followed by Pythagoras. Each basic direction \(v_i\) in S is stretched by \(\sigma_i\ge\sigma_{k+1}\), and the images \(\sigma_iu_i\) are perpendicular, so nothing cancels when they are combined. That is why every unit vector in S, not only the \(v_i\), is stretched by at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\).

Example. Take \(A=\operatorname{diag}(3,2)\), so S is the whole plane and \(\sigma_2=2\). A unit vector is \(w=(a,b)\) with \(a^2+b^2=1\), and

\[ Aw=\begin{pmatrix}3a\\2b\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|Aw\|^2=9a^2+4b^2\;\ge\;4a^2+4b^2=4 . \]

So \(\|Aw\|\ge2\) for every unit w, with equality at \(w=(0,1)\). In pictures: A turns the unit circle into an ellipse with arms 3 and 2, and no point of the ellipse is closer to the origin than 2.

The perpendicular images matter. The 2 × 2 matrix with every entry 1 stretches (1, 0) and (0, 1) by \(\sqrt2\) each, yet sends \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\) to zero, because their images are equal and cancel. Compare the two in Explorer 2.

A rank-k matrix sends some combination of k + 1 directions to zero (Step 2)

Why we want \(Bw=0\). For a general input, \((A-B)w=Aw-Bw\), and Bw is an unknown vector that might nearly cancel Aw. Rank is the only thing we know about B. Where \(Bw=0\), the error is exactly Aw, which Step 1 lets us measure.

Step 2 uses one fact: a space of dimension k cannot hold more than k independent vectors. The space here is the column space of B, the set of all its outputs Bx, whose dimension is the rank of B.

Example. This B has rank one, because every output is a multiple of (1, 2, 0). Feed it \(v_1=(1,0,0)\) and \(v_2=(0,1,0)\):

\[ B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\2&2&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Bv_1=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad Bv_2=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The two outputs are equal, so \(Bv_1-Bv_2=0\), which means \(c_1=1\) and \(c_2=-1\). The matching input is \(w=(v_1-v_2)/\sqrt2=(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2\), and \(Bw=0\).

Finding the c’s is a linear system. Put \(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\) in the columns of a matrix V. The equation \(c_1Bv_1+\cdots+c_{k+1}Bv_{k+1}=0\) is the homogeneous system \((BV)c=0\). BV has \(k+1\) columns but rank at most k, so elimination leaves a free variable, and a free variable gives a nonzero solution.

The whole proof on one example

With \(A=\operatorname{diag}(3,2,1)\) and \(k=1\), no rank-one B gets the error below \(\sigma_2=2\).

\[ A=\begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad A_1=\begin{pmatrix}3&0&0\\0&0&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|A-A_1\|_2=\sigma_2=2 . \]

Here \(v_1,v_2,v_3\) are the x-, y- and z-axes, so S is the xy-plane.

  • Step 1. A stretches every unit vector in the xy-plane by at least 2: for \(w=(a,b,0)\), \(\|Aw\|^2=9a^2+4b^2\ge4\).
  • Step 2. For any rank-one B, the outputs \(Bv_1\) and \(Bv_2\) lie on B’s single output line, so \(c_1Bv_1+c_2Bv_2=0\) for some \(c_1,c_2\) not both zero. Then \(w=(c_1,c_2,0)\), scaled to length 1, satisfies \(Bw=0\) and \(\|Aw\|\ge2\).

With the rank-one B from Step 2’s example. There \(Bv_1=Bv_2\), so \(w=(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2\) and

\[ Bw=0,\qquad (A-B)w=Aw=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}3\\-2\\0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \|(A-B)w\|=\sqrt{13/2}\approx 2.55\;\ge\;2 . \]

The 2-norm itself is larger still, \(\|A-B\|_2\approx2.92\). The proof needs only the lower bound from w.

With \(B=A_1\). Now \(Bv_1=(3,0,0)\) and \(Bv_2=0\), so \(c_1=0\), \(c_2=1\) and \(w=(0,1,0)\). Then \(\|Aw\|=2\) exactly. The best opponent pushes w onto A’s weakest direction in S, which is why the bound \(\sigma_2\) cannot be improved.

The picture. The w that Step 2 finds lies in two planes at once. One is the null space of B, the inputs B sends to zero, which is a plane: B uses one direction of \(\mathbb{R}^3\), so it ignores the other two. The other is S, the xy-plane. Two planes through the origin in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) always share a line, and w points along it. Questions that come up

Couldn’t some other vector have \(\|(A-B)x\|\) smaller than \(\sigma_{k+1}\)?

Yes, and that is fine. The 2-norm is the largest stretch, so one input stretched by \(\sigma_{k+1}\) settles it. In the example, \(A-A_1\) sends the x-axis to zero, yet its norm is 2.

Aren’t we just picking a w that agrees with what we want?

No, because B comes first and is arbitrary. Step 2 builds w from B itself, out of the dependence among \(Bv_1,\dots,Bv_{k+1}\), and that dependence exists for every rank-k B, including the best one anyone could design. In Explorer 4 you choose B; the page only finds w afterwards.

Why \(k+1\) directions and not k?

Because k outputs in a k-dimensional space can be independent, and then Step 2 finds nothing. \(A_k\) is exactly that case: it sends \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) to \(\sigma_1u_1,\dots,\sigma_ku_k\), which are independent when \(\sigma_k>0\), so no combination of \(v_1,\dots,v_k\) goes to zero. With \(k+1\) directions the outputs must be dependent, and the weakest of those directions is stretched by \(\sigma_{k+1}\). That is where the number in the theorem comes from.

Is \(\sigma_{k+1}\) really the best bound?

Yes, because truncation achieves it. For \(B=A_k\), the dependence is simply \(A_kv_{k+1}=0\), so \(w=v_{k+1}\) and \(\|(A-A_k)w\|=\sigma_{k+1}\).

Why not use the triangle inequality instead?

You could write \(\|Aw-Bw\|\ge\|Aw\|-\|Bw\|\), but \(\|Bw\|\) is unknown and might be large, which makes the bound useless. The best case is \(\|Bw\|=0\), and Step 2 gives exactly that.

Check your understanding

Work each one out first, then open the answer.

1. Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(5,4,2)\) and \(k=1\). What does the theorem say about \(\|A-B\|_2\) for every rank-one B? Which matrix achieves it?

\(\|A-B\|_2\ge\sigma_2=4\) for every rank-one B. The truncation \(A_1=\operatorname{diag}(5,0,0)\) achieves it: \(A-A_1=\operatorname{diag}(0,4,2)\), whose largest stretch is 4.

2. For that A, let B be the matrix with a single 1 in the top-left corner and zeros elsewhere. Compute \(Bv_1\) and \(Bv_2\), find \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) with \(c_1Bv_1+c_2Bv_2=0\), and use them to find w and \(\|(A-B)w\|\).

\(Bv_1=(1,0,0)\) and \(Bv_2=0\), so \(c_1=0\), \(c_2=1\) works, and \(w=v_2=(0,1,0)\). Then \(Bw=0\) and \((A-B)w=Aw=(0,4,0)\), so \(\|(A-B)w\|=4\ge\sigma_2\).

3. Let \(A=\operatorname{diag}(3,2,1)\) and let B have rows (1, 2, 0), (1, 2, 0) and (0, 0, 0). Find the dependence between \(Bv_1\) and \(Bv_2\), build w, and check that \(\|(A-B)w\|\ge2\).

\(Bv_1=(1,1,0)\) and \(Bv_2=(2,2,0)\), so \(2Bv_1-Bv_2=0\): \(c_1=2\), \(c_2=-1\). Then \(w=(2,-1,0)/\sqrt5\), \(Bw=0\), and \(Aw=(6,-2,0)/\sqrt5\), so \(\|(A-B)w\|=\sqrt{40/5}=2\sqrt2\approx2.83\ge2\).

4. Explain in your own words why the proof uses \(v_1,\dots,v_{k+1}\) rather than \(v_1,\dots,v_k\).

B has rank at most k, so it can keep k directions apart: \(Bv_1,\dots,Bv_k\) may be independent, and \(A_k\) shows they can be. Only with \(k+1\) directions are the outputs forced to be dependent, which produces the w with \(Bw=0\).

5. A classmate says: “I found a vector x with \(\|(A-B)x\|=0\), so \(\|A-B\|_2\) could be 0.” What is wrong with this reasoning?

\(\|A-B\|_2\) is the largest stretch over all unit inputs. One input with stretch 0 says nothing about the largest; Explorer 1 with diag(0, 2) shows a matrix that sends a direction to 0 and still has norm 2. The proof produces a different input w with stretch at least \(\sigma_{k+1}\), and that is enough.
