Layers of a picture

A grayscale image is a matrix. Its SVD splits it into rank-one layers, largest first, and Eckart–Young says the first k layers are the best rank-k image there is. Slide k and watch the picture come back, and check that the singular values predict the error before you look.

Keep the first k layers

Your image stays on your computer: it is shrunk to about 150 pixels on its longer side and processed in this page.

Singular values, largest first (log scale). Blue: the layers kept. Gray: the layers thrown away. By Eckart–Young, the gray ones are the error.

Predicting the error

By Eckart–Young, the relative Frobenius error of Ak depends only on the singular values you throw away:

‖A − Ak‖F / ‖A‖F  =  square root of  (σk+12 + σk+22 + ···) / (σ12 + σ22 + ···).

The readout shows this prediction next to the error measured pixel by pixel. Use the prediction to answer the question a compression program has to answer: how few layers can I keep?

Pick a target and the slider jumps to the answer, found from the singular values alone.

Things to try

  • Plaid. Before you move the slider: how many layers does it need? (It is a horizontal pattern plus a vertical one.) What happens to the singular values after that?
  • Straight vs. diagonal stripes. Same stripes, turned 45°. Why does the diagonal version need so many more layers? Look at “layer k alone”: every layer is a column pattern times a row pattern.
  • The photograph. Find the smallest k you would call recognizable. What does that cost in storage?
  • The error picture. Where in the photograph is the error concentrated at k = 10? Why there?
