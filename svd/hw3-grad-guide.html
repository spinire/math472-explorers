From Weyl to Eckart–Young

A guided walk through the starred problem: proving that keeping the first \(k\) SVD layers is also the best rank-\(k\) approximation in the Frobenius norm.

You already know the 2-norm version of Eckart–Young. Its proof finds a single vector \(w\) that any rank-\(k\) competitor misses, which controls only the largest singular value of the error. The Frobenius norm adds up the squares of all the singular values, so we need a statement about every \(\sigma_i\) at once. That is the new tool, Weyl's inequality, and after it the proof takes two short steps. This page gives you the facts you will use, lets you test each inequality on real matrices, and offers hints in stages. It does not give the full write-up; that is your job.

  1. Part (a)Prove Weyl's inequality \(\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y)\le\sigma_i(X)+\sigma_j(Y)\).
  2. Part (b)Use it to compare the singular values of \(A\) and \(A-B\), shifted by \(k\).
  3. Part (c)Square and add to get the Frobenius statement.

The facts you will use

Every step of the proof is one of these facts applied once. If a step feels stuck, come back here and ask which fact it needs. Throughout, \(\sigma_i(M)\) is the \(i\)-th singular value of \(M\), and \(M_k=\sigma_1u_1v_1^T+\cdots+\sigma_ku_kv_k^T\) keeps the first \(k\) layers.

F1The min form of Eckart–Young

\[\sigma_{k+1}(M)=\min_{\operatorname{rank}(B)\le k}\|M-B\|_2 .\]

This is the 2-norm theorem from the notes in one line: every \(B\) of rank at most \(k\) has \(\|M-B\|_2\ge\sigma_{k+1}\), and \(B=M_k\) achieves it. In words: \(\sigma_{k+1}(M)\) is the distance from \(M\) to the nearest matrix of rank at most \(k\). The useful consequence is an upper bound: for any particular \(B\) of rank at most \(k\),

\[\sigma_{k+1}(M)\le\|M-B\|_2 .\]

The smallest value is no bigger than any one value. You never need to find the minimizer; one good choice of \(B\) is enough.

F2What truncation costs

\[\|M-M_k\|_2=\sigma_{k+1}(M).\]

The leftover \(M-M_k=\sigma_{k+1}u_{k+1}v_{k+1}^T+\cdots\) is itself an SVD, and its largest stretch is its largest singular value. With \(k=0\), \(M_0=0\) and this says \(\|M\|_2=\sigma_1(M)\).

F3Rank adds at most

\[\operatorname{rank}(P+Q)\le\operatorname{rank}(P)+\operatorname{rank}(Q).\]

Every column of \(P+Q\) is a column of \(P\) plus a column of \(Q\), so it lies in \(C(P)+C(Q)\), a space of dimension at most \(\operatorname{rank}P+\operatorname{rank}Q\). Also, \(M_k\) is a sum of \(k\) rank-one layers, so \(\operatorname{rank}(M_k)\le k\).

F4Frobenius norm from singular values

\[\|M\|_F^2=\sigma_1(M)^2+\sigma_2(M)^2+\cdots .\]

Multiplying by orthonormal \(U\) and \(V^T\) does not change the sum of squared entries, so \(\|M\|_F^2=\|\Sigma\|_F^2\).

F5Zero past the rank

If \(M\) has rank \(r\), we set \(\sigma_i(M)=0\) for \(i>r\). So a matrix of rank at most \(k\) has \(\sigma_{k+1}=0\). With this convention F1 and F2 still hold for every \(k\).

Part (a): Weyl's inequality

For matrices \(X\) and \(Y\) of the same shape, and any \(i,j\ge1\):

\[\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y)\ \le\ \sigma_i(X)+\sigma_j(Y).\]

What it says. Start with the simplest case, \(i=j=1\): \(\sigma_1(X+Y)\le\sigma_1(X)+\sigma_1(Y)\). Since \(\sigma_1\) is the 2-norm, that is just the triangle inequality \(\|X+Y\|_2\le\|X\|_2+\|Y\|_2\). Weyl's inequality extends this to the other singular values: adding \(Y\) can raise singular values, but in a controlled way. Taking \(j=1\) gives \(\sigma_i(X+Y)\le\sigma_i(X)+\|Y\|_2\): no singular value moves up by more than \(\|Y\|_2\).

The plan of the proof is a chain of three comparisons, with \(B=X_{i-1}+Y_{j-1}\):

\[\begin{aligned}\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y)\ &\overset{\text{(ii)}}{\le}\ \|X+Y-B\|_2\\ &\overset{\text{(iii)}}{\le}\ \sigma_i(X)+\sigma_j(Y),\end{aligned}\]

and step (i) checks that \(B\) is allowed in the first comparison. The lab below computes all three numbers for random matrices, so you can watch the chain hold before you prove it.

Building the proof

Warm-up. Check that \(i=j=1\) is the triangle inequality. Which fact turns \(\sigma_1\) into a norm?

Hint

F2 with \(k=0\): \(\|M\|_2=\sigma_1(M)\).

(i) Explain why \(B=X_{i-1}+Y_{j-1}\) has rank at most \(i+j-2\).

Hint 1

\(X_{i-1}\) is a sum of how many rank-one layers? What is its rank, at most?

Hint 2

Combine \(\operatorname{rank}X_{i-1}\le i-1\) and \(\operatorname{rank}Y_{j-1}\le j-1\) with F3.

(ii) Use the min form to show \(\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y)\le\|X+Y-B\|_2\).

Hint 1

Apply F1 to the matrix \(M=X+Y\). Which value of \(k\) makes \(\sigma_{k+1}\) equal to \(\sigma_{i+j-1}\)?

Hint 2

With \(k=i+j-2\), F1 says \(\sigma_{i+j-1}(X+Y)\) is the smallest distance from \(X+Y\) to a matrix of rank at most \(i+j-2\). Step (i) says \(B\) is one of those matrices. The smallest distance is at most the distance to \(B\).

(iii) Split \(X+Y-B\) into two pieces and finish with the triangle inequality and F2.

Hint 1

\(X+Y-B=(X-X_{i-1})+(Y-Y_{j-1})\).

Hint 2

Each piece is a truncation error. F2 with \(k=i-1\) gives \(\|X-X_{i-1}\|_2=\sigma_i(X)\); do the same for \(Y\).

Edge cases

If \(i=1\), then \(X_0=0\) and the piece is \(X\) itself, with \(\|X\|_2=\sigma_1(X)\). If \(i-1\) is at least the rank of \(X\), then \(X_{i-1}=X\) and \(\sigma_i(X)=0\) by F5; the piece is zero and the step still holds. Say this in one sentence in your write-up.

Part (b): shifting by \(k\)

Now let \(B\) be any matrix of rank at most \(k\), the competitor we want to beat. Apply Weyl's inequality with \(X=A-B\), \(Y=B\) and \(j=k+1\). Then \(X+Y=A\), and the claim is

\[\sigma_{i+k}(A)\ \le\ \sigma_i(A-B)\qquad\text{for every } i\ge1 .\]

Read it as: the error matrix \(A-B\) has singular values at least as large as those of \(A\), once you skip the first \(k\) of \(A\)'s. A rank-\(k\) matrix can absorb at most \(k\) of \(A\)'s singular values; the rest show up in the error.

Before you hand it in

  • Every \(\le\) and \(=\) in your proof names the fact it uses (F1–F5, or the triangle inequality).
  • Part (a) says where the rank bound from (i) is used.
  • Part (a) handles \(i=1\) and \(i-1\ge\operatorname{rank}X\) in a sentence.
  • Part (b) says why \(\sigma_{k+1}(B)=0\).
  • Part (c) says where the Frobenius formula F4 is used, on both sides.

Common trap. In step (ii), trying to show that \(B\) is the closest rank-\((i+j-2)\) matrix to \(X+Y\). It usually is not, and you do not need it to be. F1 gives an upper bound for any \(B\) of the right rank.

Common trap. In part (b), applying Weyl with \(X=A\) and \(Y=-B\). That bounds the singular values of \(A-B\) from above, the wrong direction. Put the unknown error \(A-B\) in the role of \(X\).
